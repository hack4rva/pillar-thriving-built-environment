**PILLAR: BUILT ENVIRONMENT\
Workshop Discussion Summary**

**Targeted Problem Statement Discussion**

Several candidate targeted problems were reviewed during the session, including:

- Real-time visibility of DPW fleet operations

- Centralized transportation project information and discoverability

- Transportation project transparency and feedback visibility

- Sustainable Design Standards (SDS) integration in capital projects

<!-- -->

- Potential real-world adoption

Three themes emerged:

**1. Strong energy around transportation project visibility**

Participants repeatedly returned to the idea that residents struggle to understand:

- What infrastructure projects are happening

- Where they are happening

- Why they are happening

- What stage they are in

Project information currently exists across multiple systems, including ArcGIS maps, grant documentation, and program pages, making it difficult for residents to locate and interpret. The group saw an opportunity for a tool that could:

- Aggregate existing project data

- Translate technical project descriptions

- Allow residents to search by address or neighborhood

- Show project timelines and progress

Participants believed a solution in this space could significantly improve public understanding and trust in transportation investments.

**2. Fleet visibility was seen as highly practical and solvable**

The discussion also highlighted operational challenges around real-time fleet visibility, particularly for services like snow removal and street cleaning. Currently:

- Route tracking is largely manual

- Supervisors do not have real-time route completion visibility

- Public updates require manual compilation

Meanwhile, residents frequently want to know when their street will be serviced. Because GPS devices are already being installed across the fleet, participants felt that technology could realistically help bridge this gap. The group noted that even partial improvements---such as route completion updates rather than live vehicle tracking---could significantly improve both internal coordination and public communication.

**3. Sustainability integration was seen as important but less hackable**

The Sustainable Design Standards (SDS) problem was viewed as strategically important but potentially difficult for hackathon participants to solve. Participants noted that:

- SDS implementation requires more internal definition and policy clarity

- Departments operate with separate workflows and funding structures

- Sustainability goals still need clearer operational translation

The group ultimately felt this topic might require more internal city work before a hackathon solution could be effective, even though integrating sustainability earlier in capital projects is a significant long-term challenge.

**Additional themes surfaced during brainstorming**

The workshop also generated several adjacent observations about the built environment, including:

- Car-centric street design and safety concerns

- Incomplete tree inventory data and vacant tree wells

- Opportunities to incentivize sustainable behaviors (e.g., carpooling)

- The importance of making sustainability work more visible to residents

These ideas ultimately fed more into the Blue Sky discussion than into the targeted problem statements.

**BLUE SKY DISCUSSION SUMMARY**

**Making infrastructure work visible**

Many infrastructure improvements are invisible to residents, especially projects underground or inside long-term planning processes. Participants noted that residents often assume "nothing is happening" even when significant work is underway.

**Making sustainability tangible**

Several participants emphasized that sustainability and resilience goals are often difficult for residents to see or understand. There was interest in tools that could make sustainability outcomes visible, such as:

- tree canopy growth

- stormwater projects

- climate resilience investments

**Rebalancing car-centric urban design**

Participants discussed the prevalence of car-centric infrastructure and the opportunity to rethink intersections, street safety, and mobility patterns.

These discussions connected to Vision Zero goals and pedestrian safety concerns.

**Improving environmental data visibility**

Participants also discussed challenges around environmental data, including:

- outdated tree inventory data

- vacant tree wells across the city

- lack of accessible environmental metrics

Better data visibility could support stronger policy decisions and community engagement.

**PILLAR: THRIVING BUILT ENVIRONMENT\
REVISED Targeted Problem Statement: Transportation Project Visibility & Discoverability**

**Problem Statement**

How might we use technology to improve how Richmond residents find and understand transportation and infrastructure projects happening in their neighborhoods so that project information is clear, centralized, and easy to track?

**Context**

The Department of Public Works manages numerous transportation projects across Richmond, including safety improvements, streetscape upgrades, grant-funded initiatives, and long-term mobility programs. Information about these projects currently exists across multiple platforms, including ArcGIS maps, program pages, grant documentation, and internal project tracking tools. Residents who want to understand what projects are happening in their neighborhood often struggle to locate information or interpret technical descriptions. As a result, residents frequently ask: What projects are happening near me? What stage are they in? Why was this location prioritized? When will the work be finished?

Improving the visibility and clarity of transportation project information could significantly improve public understanding, reduce repetitive information requests, and strengthen trust in infrastructure investments.

**Constraints**

- Existing systems such as ArcGIS project maps must remain in place

- Project information must remain technically accurate

- Staff capacity for manual updates is limited

- Some project details evolve during design and permitting phases

- Budget constraints limit development of large custom platforms

**Success Would Mean**

- Residents can search by address or neighborhood

- All nearby infrastructure projects appear in one place

- Project status is clearly visible (planned, design, construction, complete)

- Technical descriptions are translated into plain language

- Staff receive fewer repetitive information requests

EXAMPLE IDEAS HACKATHON TEAMS MIGHT COME UP WITH\
\
**City Infrastructure Explorer Map:** A public map that aggregates data from multiple city systems and displays all active infrastructure and transportation projects with plain-language descriptions, project status, timelines, and explanations of why the work is being done.

**"What's Happening Here?" Mobile Lookup Tool:** A smartphone tool that allows residents to take a photo of a construction site or enter an address and instantly receive project details including purpose, timeline, responsible department, and current status. AND, a simple SMS service where residents text an address, intersection, or nearby landmark and receive an automated response with project information, status updates, and links to more details. AND an opt-in system that sends residents notifications when infrastructure projects begin, change stages, or are completed within a selected neighborhood.

**Targeted Problem Statement Option 2\
Fleet Operations Visibility & Public Communication**

**Problem Statement**

How might we use technology to improve how Richmond residents and DPW staff track the progress of services like snow removal and street cleaning so that operations are easier to coordinate and residents receive timely, accurate updates?

**Context**

The Department of Public Works manages highly visible operational services such as snow removal and street cleaning. During storms and seasonal cleaning cycles, residents frequently seek information about when their street will be serviced. Currently:

- Route progress reporting is largely manual

- Supervisors lack real-time visibility into route completion

- Public updates must be manually compiled by staff back at the office

Meanwhile, GPS devices are being installed across the fleet, creating new opportunities to improve operational coordination and automate public updates. Better visibility into route progress could reduce staff reporting burdens, improve internal coordination, and give residents clearer expectations during service periods. Additionally, the city has yet to consider how employee's city-owned mobile phones could act as a tool for data and status capture for street activity.

**Constraints**

- GPS infrastructure is still being implemented

- Public tools should avoid exposing sensitive

- Staffing capacity for manual oversight is limited

**Success Would Mean**

- Supervisors can see route progress without manual check-ins

- Public updates are automated or significantly streamlined

- Residents can see when their street is scheduled or completed

- Fewer unexpected tickets or towing during street cleaning

- Improved public confidence during snowstorms and service cycles

**Street Service Status Map:** A map showing whether a street is scheduled, in progress, or completed for services like snow removal or street cleaning---without displaying exact vehicle locations. A visualization tool that shows which neighborhoods have already received services and which areas are still scheduled, helping residents and staff understand progress during major events like snowstorms.

**Fleet Route Progress Dashboard:** A backend system that converts GPS or phone signals and worker input data into automated route-completion updates for both internal staff and public-facing service status maps. This would support an internal operations dashboard that shows supervisors which routes are complete, in progress, or delayed based on GPS signals from fleet vehicles.
